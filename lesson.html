Reading: Should We Save Someone's Life or Destroy It?

One Sabbath when Jesus and his disciples were walking through some wheat fields, the disciples picked some wheat. On another Sabbath Jesus was teaching in a synagogue, and a man with a paralyzed right hand was there.

Some Pharisees and teachers of the Law of Moses kept watching Jesus to see if he would heal the man. They did this because they wanted to accuse Jesus of doing something wrong.

Jesus knew what they were thinking, so he told the man to stand up where everyone could see him. And the man stood up. Then Jesus asked, “On the Sabbath should we do good deeds or evil deeds? Should we save someone's life or destroy it?”

Reference: Luke 6:6–11 | Canadian Contemporary English Version

The Four Principles of Biomedical Ethics

1. Autonomy

Respecting a person's right to make informed and voluntary decisions concerning their own body and healthcare. In the passage, the man's participation and response to Jesus' instruction can prompt discussion about personal agency. The text does not establish all the requirements of modern informed consent.

2. Beneficence

The obligation to act in ways that benefit patients and promote their health and well-being. Jesus' healing of the man can be discussed as an act of doing good and improving well-being.

3. Non-maleficence

The obligation to avoid causing unnecessary or unjustified harm. Jesus' question about doing evil and destroying life invites reflection on harm and the consequences of withholding help.

4. Justice

Treating people fairly and ensuring equitable access to healthcare and resources. The man's condition should not make him undeserving of help. The passage invites reflection on fair treatment and human welfare.

The Hippocratic Oath

The Hippocratic Oath is an ancient ethical declaration traditionally associated with Hippocrates, a Greek physician who lived around 460–370 BCE. It expresses professional commitments concerning patient welfare, avoiding harm, confidentiality and professional responsibility.

Primum non nocere

“First, do no harm.”

This Latin expression summarizes an important idea in medical ethics, but it does not appear verbatim in the original Hippocratic Oath.

How the ideas relate

The Hippocratic Oath is a historical expression of medical professional ethics. The four principles are a modern framework for analyzing biomedical dilemmas. They are related, but they are not interchangeable.

Knowledge assessment

Choose one answer for each question. This is a static preview of the original assessment. Answers cannot currently be submitted, graded or saved on this GitHub Pages demonstration.

1. The man responds to Jesus' instruction and participates in the encounter. Which principle is most closely related to personal agency?

2. Jesus heals the man, promoting his well-being. Which principle is illustrated?

3. Jesus questions whether refusing to help someone could contribute to harm. Which principle is relevant?

4. The man's disability should not make him less deserving of assistance. Which principle is most closely illustrated?

5. A doctor protects a patient's private medical information. Which Hippocratic commitment does this reflect?

6. A patient refuses surgery after understanding its risks and benefits. The doctor respects the decision. Which principle applies?

7. A hospital allocates limited ICU beds using fair medical criteria rather than wealth. Which principle is demonstrated?

8. A surgeon avoids an unnecessary procedure because its risks are not justified. Which principle is applied?
